# EEP 8 - How Edison adopts an outside standard

## Abstract

Four EEPs adopt a text from outside Edison: EEP 1 adopts PEP 1, EEP 3 adopts
Jekyll's front-matter format, EEP 5 adopts Google's code of conduct, and EEP 6
adopts Google's Python style guide. EEP 5 states the shape they share: "A pin, a
rule of adoption, a substitution table, and the decisions adoption forced."

The shape is real, and it has been written out four times. Eleven passages
recur near-verbatim across the four, the rule of adoption exists in three
different wordings that nothing checks for agreement, and two of the three
substitution tables are verified by nothing at all.

This document writes the repeated half once. An adoption EEP then carries only
what is particular to it: which text, at which revision, why that one and not
another, the substitutions it needs, and the decisions adoption forced.

It also makes the pin machine-readable. Every adoption states its revision in
one sentence of a fixed form, so a tool can find every adoption in the series
and check that each pinned file still resolves, rather than one tool being
hardcoded to one EEP.

Nothing here changes what any existing EEP adopts.

## Motivation

The owner has asked for a survey of every domain Edison must hold a position
on, and intends to adopt rather than invent wherever a standard exists. The
survey found more than seventy domains. If even a tenth of them are adopted,
the series gains seven more adoption EEPs.

At four, the repetition is a smell. At eleven it is a maintenance problem, and
two symptoms of it are already visible.

The first is drift between copies of the same rule. EEP 1's rule of adoption
says a vacant step "stays in the process, it is skipped for now, and it is
filled as soon as it can be". EEP 5 and EEP 6 restate it in their own words,
one of them dropping "for now", and each appends a back-reference counting
which document it is: "applied to a different document", "applied to a third
document". That counting has no sensible answer at the tenth adoption, and
nothing checks that the three wordings still say the same thing.

The second is that the checking is hardcoded. `tools/check-adoption` reads the
pin out of EEP 1, fetches PEP 1, and verifies that EEP 1's substitution table
covers every term the pinned text uses. It cannot be pointed at any other EEP
without being edited: the repository path, the document, the raw URL and the
list of named terms are all constants in it. EEP 5's and EEP 6's substitution
tables are checked by nothing, and `tools/check-eeps` does not know the words
pin, adoption or substitution.

EEP 3's adoption of Jekyll shows what that costs. It is a single paragraph with
no pin heading, no rule of adoption, no substitution table, no committed date
and no drift sentence, and it passes every check in the repository.

## Specification

### What an adoption is

An **adoption** is a decision that a text published outside Edison governs some
part of how Edison works, whole and at one exact revision, read through a table
of substitutions.

An **adoption EEP** is an EEP that makes one. This document binds every
adoption EEP, and binds nothing else.

Adopting is not citing. An EEP may quote or cite an outside document without
adopting it, and EEP 1 does exactly that with PEP 13. A citation carries no
pin, states no substitutions, and binds nobody.

### What an adoption EEP contains

Inside `## Specification`, in this order, and each spelled as shown:

| Heading | What it holds |
|---|---|
| `### The pin` | the pin sentence and the drift sentence |
| `### Why this one` | the ranking comparison |
| `### What it binds` | only when the adoption reaches past the series |
| `### The rule of adoption` | the sentence below, verbatim |
| `### Substitutions` | the table |
| any further headings | the decisions adoption forced |

`### What it binds` is required only of an adoption that reaches past the EEP
series, as EEP 6's does. An adoption that governs only the series omits it.

### The pin sentence

One sentence, in this form, so that a tool can find it:

```text
at revision `<40 hexadecimal characters>` of `<path>` in
`github.com/<owner>/<repository>`, committed <DD-MMM-YYYY>
```

The sentence may be introduced however reads best; EEP 1 writes "Edison follows
PEP 1 as it stands at revision", EEP 5 writes "Edison adopts the Code of
Conduct at revision". What is fixed is everything from `at revision` onward.

The committed date is required. It is what tells a reader how old the adopted
text is without fetching it.

Where the adopted text does not live in a git repository, there is no pin in
this form and the adoption cannot be written under this document. Say so, and
bring the case to the council rather than inventing a second form.

### The drift sentence

Immediately after the pin:

```text
Later changes to <the adopted text> do not change Edison's <what it
governs> until the pin is moved.
```

This is what a pin means, and all three adoptions that carry it say it in
almost these words. It is written here so the fourth does not have to invent
its own.

### The rule of adoption

Every adoption is read with this rule. An adoption EEP reproduces it verbatim
rather than restating it:

> The pinned text is read with the substitutions below. Nothing is added and
> nothing is removed. Where a substitute does not exist, the clause is
> **vacant**: it stays in the text, it is skipped for now, and it is filled as
> soon as it can be.

**Vacant** has that meaning throughout the series, and this is where it is
defined. EEP 1 defines it inside its own rule of adoption, which is why every
later adoption has had to point back at EEP 1 to borrow it.

### Why this one

EEP 2 ranks what may confirm a thing, and says that when two disagree, "the
higher one that has spoken is followed". An adoption EEP must therefore show
its work: name the candidates it weighed, say where each publisher sits on that
ranking, and show that the ranking chose, not preference.

EEP 5 and EEP 6 both do this already. EEP 6 puts it plainly: "The ranking is
doing the work, not preference."

Where two candidates sit at the same rank, the ranking does not choose and the
council does. The resolution records which considerations decided it.

Where nothing above rank 4 has spoken and no community practice has either, say
so. An absence is a finding, and recording it is what stops the next agent
inventing something to fill it.

### Substitutions

A table of two columns: what the pinned text says, and what it is read as.

Every term the pinned text uses that has no Edison answer needs a row, and a
row may read `vacant`. A term with no row is a term the reader has to guess at,
which is what EEP 1's table was doing for seven terms before anything checked
it.

### Where the source and its own tool disagree

The source governs and the tool has a defect.

This is written twice in the series and settled nowhere. EEP 1: "Where this
document and the pinned PEP 1 seem to differ on process, PEP 1 is right and
this document has a defect." EEP 6, of Google's style guide against Google's
own linter: "the guide is right and the tool has a defect, which is the rule
EEP 1 already applies to itself against PEP 1."

It follows that the disagreement is recorded rather than resolved silently, and
that a tool configured to depart from its source names each departure where it
occurs.

### Licence, and when a working copy is allowed

An adoption EEP records the licence of the text it adopts, because the licence
decides whether Edison may carry a copy of it.

A working copy is allowed only where something has to read the file locally.
`tools/pylintrc` is the only one in the series today. Where a working copy
exists, it names the revision it was taken from and every divergence from it,
and something refuses a divergence that is not recorded.

Everything else is adopted by reference and not reproduced. A restatement
drifts from its original in silence; a pin shows drift plainly.

### Moving a pin

Moving a pin is a change to the adoption EEP, and the council decides it, which
is the clause EEP 1 already applies to itself.

The change history records the revision moved from, the revision moved to, and
what changed in the adopted text that made the move worth making.

### What is checked, and what is not

`tools/check-eeps` rule `E017` refuses an EEP that has a `### The pin` heading
but no pin sentence, no drift sentence or no substitution table.

`tools/check-adoption` finds every pin sentence in the series and checks that
each pinned file still resolves at its pinned revision. It needs a network and
says so when it has none, as it already does.

What is not checked: that a substitution table is complete. That is checked for
EEP 1 only, because the terms of the pinned PEP 1 can be extracted
mechanically. Completeness for any other adoption is an editor's job, and this
document does not pretend otherwise.

## Rationale

The shape is not invented here. It is read off four documents that already have
it, and the one place this document adds anything, the required ranking
comparison, is EEP 2's rule applied to a step that two of the four already
perform.

Writing it once rather than four more times follows EEP 2's second protected
thing, simplicity, and it removes the specific failure the series has already
suffered: EEP 1's `PEP 12` row said the template was vacant for four commits
after EEP 3 filled it. That was a copy of a fact drifting from the fact.
Eleven copies of a rule can drift the same way.

Making the pin machine-readable is the part that pays for itself. The sentence
form is not a new convention; three of four adoptions already write it exactly
this way, and the fourth is missing only its committed date. Fixing the form
costs nothing and turns a hardcoded one-document check into one that finds
every adoption in the series.

This document deliberately does not generalise the completeness check. EEP 1's
terms can be extracted because PEP 1 marks its cross-references; Google's style
guide has no such structure. Claiming otherwise would put a check in the
repository that quietly proves nothing, which is the failure EEP 6 already
records about a green run without `pylint`.

## Backwards compatibility

This document describes four existing adoptions, and one of them does not meet
it.

EEP 1, EEP 5 and EEP 6 carry the pin sentence, the drift sentence, the rule of
adoption and a substitution table. Their wordings of the rule differ from each
other and from the one above, and bringing them into line is a change to three
Active EEPs that the council decides. Nothing here changes what they adopt.

EEP 3's adoption of Jekyll has a pin sentence and nothing else: no committed
date, no drift sentence, no rule of adoption, no substitution table, and no
`### The pin` heading. Rule `E017` does not fire on it, because it has no pin
heading to fire on. That is recorded as an open issue rather than repaired
here, because repairing it means deciding what Jekyll's substitutions are, and
that is a separate question from this document's.

## How to teach this

Tell an author: you are adopting something, so read EEP 8 first. It gives you
the headings, the pin sentence, the drift sentence and the rule of adoption to
copy verbatim. What you have to write yourself is why this text and not
another, by EEP 2's ranking; the substitutions; and what adopting it forced you
to decide.

The first adoption EEP written to this document. The survey of domains the

## Rejected ideas

- **A template file to copy from.** The same objection EEP 3 raised against
  copying PEP 12, and EEP 1 against copying PEP 1: a copy drifts from its
  original in silence. A document that says the rule once, and a check that
  refuses an adoption which does not follow it, does not drift.
- **Generalising the completeness check to every adoption.** It cannot be done
  honestly. PEP 1 marks its cross-references so its terms can be harvested;
  nothing else adopted so far does. A check that ran over every table and found
  nothing would be worse than no check, because it would look like coverage.
- **Making this a section of EEP 7 instead.** EEP 7 states the pin-not-copy
  rule for every repository, and this document is about how one EEP is written,
  which is the series' own business. EEP 7 is also still Draft, so a rule
  placed in it would bind nothing.
- **Repairing EEP 3's adoption in this document.** It needs a substitution
  table for Jekyll's front-matter format, which is a question about Jekyll and
  not about adoption. Raised as an open issue.
- **Numbering adoptions.** Considered as a way to replace "applied to a third
  document". A register that has to be renumbered as documents are accepted and
  rejected is one more thing to be wrong; the back-reference is simply deleted
  instead, since this document now holds the rule.

## Open issues

- **Three Active EEPs state the rule of adoption in their own words.** Bringing
  EEP 1, EEP 5 and EEP 6 onto the wording above is a change the council
  decides, and it is not made here.
- **EEP 3's adoption of Jekyll does not meet this document**, and no check
  fires on it. What Jekyll's substitutions are has never been asked.
- **Substitution-table completeness is checked for one adoption only.** Every
  other table rests on an editor having read it.
- **Whether an adoption may pin something that is not in a git repository.**
  Several standards the survey found publish only a versioned PDF or a page
  with no revision at all. This document has no form for them and says to bring
  the case to the council; that is a deferral, not an answer.
- **Whether the licence of an adopted text should be checked mechanically.**
  The licences differ in ways that decide whether a working copy is lawful, and
  nothing reads them today.
